Nine ways to say one point.

The picker sits in a card over the map — four places today, sometimes twice in the same card. It has to take a typed pair, the clipboard, a plus code, your own position, a pin, a place, a hider's zone centre and a tap on the map, and hand the value back out again. Four layouts below. All four are shorter than what ships now; they disagree about where the alternatives live.

Six sources, one field, one way out. That grouping is the whole design: a source writes the field, the field parses whatever it is given, and copy hands back what the field shows. Every layout below is an argument about where the six sources sit.

Before the layouts

Two decisions all four share

They are what makes any of the four fit at all. Neither is a layout question, and both collapse three of the nine requirements into one control.

1

One field, every syntax

Parser

Typing, pasting and plus codes are not three modes — they are one field and one parser. parsePastedCoordinates already reads six syntaxes; it gains plus codes and stops being about the clipboard.

Typed pair · read as lat, lng
Pasted · degrees, minutes, seconds
Plus code · 3 m square
Longitude first — reading it the other way round
Reads
Decimal pairs, DMS, lat=…&lng=…, JSON, LatLng(…), map-app URLs, plus codes
New
Plus code decode and encode. Full 11-character codes always; a short code (9C9C+9X) resolves against the map centre
Offline
An open-location-code decoder is pure arithmetic and ships in the bundle — no lookup, so it survives a dead signal

The line under the field is the whole feedback loop. It says what the app read, in the app's own words, and it is the only place a swapped pair or a truncated code gets caught before the point lands on the map.

2

The value is a readout, not two numbers

Display

One mono string, tabular figures, in whichever format is showing. Copy hands back exactly what is on screen — so the format switch is also the copy-format switch, and there is nothing else to choose.

The leading icon is not decoration: it says where the value came from, and it is the control that changes it

Copy is a state, not a screen. The button becomes a check for a second and the hint line says so. No toast, nothing to dismiss, and it works with one thumb while the other hand holds a phone.

Choosing a source

Sheet

One sheet, six rows, ordered by how often a player reaches for them. Two rows are drills into lists that already exist; the zone row appears for hiders and is simply absent for everyone else.

Where from?

Order
Where I am, the map, the clipboard, then the two lists. Frequency, not category
Paste row
Shows what is on the clipboard where the browser allows a silent read; plain "Paste" where it does not
Absent

Why A. The card is over a map, and the map is the thing the player is actually reading. One row leaves the most of it visible, and the sheet borrows the screen only for the second it takes to choose.

What it costs. Every source is two taps, and the six are invisible until you open the sheet. A player who does not know "from a pin" exists will not discover it here.

What I would build

Recommendation

A everywhere, with C's collapse in the cut editor. They are not two designs. C's open body is A's row with the sources drawn out flat instead of raised in a sheet, so one component ships with two framings: <PointField> for a card with room, <PointField collapsible> where two of them share a card. B's rail is that same source list rendered inline, kept for the one screen with the space — and it is the right first-run behaviour if a player has never opened the sheet.

D is the one to reject, unless you want the pin card to grow. It reads beautifully in isolation and hides the map at the exact moment a player is deciding where a point goes.

Three things I guessed at

Each is drawn above under the assumption printed with it. Say the word and the drawing changes.

Which point is "zone centre"?

A hider's own hiding zone has a centre — their stop. So does a zone a seeker has drawn on the map. Both are plausibly the thing a hider wants to drop into a picker.

Drawn as: the hider's own zone, named by its stop

Short plus codes, or full ones only?

A full code (9F4MGC9C+9X) is unambiguous anywhere. A short one (9C9C+9X) is what people actually read out, and needs a reference point to resolve — the map centre is the obvious one, and is wrong if the map has been panned away.

Drawn as: both, short resolved against the map centre

Does copy follow the display format?

Copying what is on screen is the honest rule, but a plus code pasted into a mapping app that does not read them is a dead end for whoever receives it.

Drawn as: copy hands back exactly what is shown
